The morning David Lee Roth called our house.

It was seven in the morning. Mom was pouring cereal, Andrew and I were getting ready for school, and Dad still hadn't come home. David Lee Roth called from the limo to explain that he'd kept Dad out all night. Mom was not impressed. She asked if Dad was cowering in the backseat. Dave laughed and said yes. They'd been out at the Gold Club, as I learned years later. Dave called again a few days later to find out if Dad had survived.

Nile Rodgers is on the far right in this photo. Pretty cool!

Dad remembered Eddie as a friend.

When Eddie Van Halen died in 2020, Dad posted about what he was like to work with. He said Eddie treated the Warner Bros. promotion people well and respected the work they did for the band. Dad had known those guys since the early Warner Bros. days, and that was what he wanted people to remember about Eddie.

Seal almost backed out of 99X.

Leslie Fram had made room for Seal to perform live on 99X's morning show in Atlanta. He was nervous about playing solo and didn't want to do it. Dad told him how important the station was, and that they couldn't let Leslie down after she'd given him the slot. He spent time talking Seal into going on. I love this story because it says a lot about Dad's job. Yes, he was there to get records played and help artists succeed. But sometimes that meant sitting with someone who was nervous, talking them through it, and helping them find the confidence to walk into a radio studio.

Dad played guitar with Billy Gibbons.

This photo was taken in Dad's Atlanta office. The story he tells happened backstage at a ZZ Top show in Savannah. Dad was resting on some equipment cases when Billy came over. Dad joked that he was learning blues licks, so Billy handed him one of his white stage guitars and invited him to warm up. Dad was nervous enough to struggle with an E chord, but Billy kept going until they found a song they could play together. Dad even got in a lead break.

Dad and Travis, after the CMA Horizon Award.

This is Dad and Travis at Travis’s house after Travis won the CMA Horizon Award. Dad wasn’t able to attend the show, but Travis thanked him on national TV, and Dad’s phone rang for two days afterward. Friends and colleagues called to congratulate him, and Warner Bros. sent flowers to Dad and Mom.

Dad believed in “American Pie.”

Before Dad worked for Warner Bros., he was a promotion man for United Artists in Atlanta, trying to persuade radio stations to take a chance on new records. One of those records was Don McLean’s “American Pie.”

The full recording ran more than eight minutes, the tempo changed, and the cryptic lyrics left people wondering what on earth Don McLean was talking about. Even the shorter single was a hard sell. Dad kept hearing that it was too strange for Top 40 radio, but he couldn’t shake what he heard in it. He believed people would love it if someone would just give them the chance to listen.

Dad remembered taking it to Jim Davenport and music director Pete Owen at WFOM in Marietta. Jim challenged him to be sure he really believed in it. Dad said yes. They gave the song a test, the request lines lit up, and Dad carried that response to other stations around the South.

The part I love most is what happened afterward. United Artists gave WFOM a gold record for helping break the song. Jim passed it to Pete, and then the two of them put Dad’s name over the original engraving and gave it to him.

Dad kept it on his wall as a reminder of what it meant to trust his ears, and to have other people trust him.

It still amazes me that a song so many people now know by heart once needed someone to keep asking for a chance. Before it had chart numbers to prove him right, Dad had his own ears and the persistence to keep knocking on doors. He helped turn a record people were passing over into one they couldn’t ignore.

“Unchained Melody,” on I-285.

Dad spent a day taking Bobby Hatfield of the Righteous Brothers to radio interviews and press appointments around Atlanta. He had been nervous about meeting one of his favorite singers, but Bobby made him feel comfortable almost immediately.

After their appointments, Bobby asked if they could ride around and see the city before dinner. They talked about the bands they had played in when they were younger, and Dad mentioned that Bobby’s version of “Unchained Melody” was his favorite song.

According to Dad, Bobby began singing the whole thing right there in the car on I-285.

Dad spent years helping other people hear records. That afternoon, he had one of his favorite voices singing beside him, with no recorder handy. He never forgot it.

Dad took “Dixie Chicken” literally.

Dad’s promotion for Little Feat’s “Dixie Chicken” came with a dress code. Lowell George was dressed as a chicken, the band were chefs, and Dad was Colonel Sanders. Together, they delivered the new record to radio stations.

Getting someone to listen to a new record could take a little imagination. Dad apparently decided it could also take poultry.

Little Feat wasn’t his first food-related promotion. The same article credits Dad with winning an Elektra award for making the rounds in a chef’s outfit with an eight-foot loaf of bread to promote Bread. Apparently, subtlety wasn’t on the menu.

It made the paper. Richard Cromelin told the story in “Record Promotion Follies: Beware Geeks Bearing Discs,” reprinted from Warner Bros.’ Waxpaper in The Drummer, September 21–28, 1976. Read the original article on page 24 ↗

His friendship with Little Feat lasted beyond that promotion. Years later, a dinner conversation with the band led Dad to connect them with Travis Tritt for “Bible Belt,” the song Travis had written for My Cousin Vinny.
